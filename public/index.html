C1 Why controls exist

Fraud needs three things. Controls take one away.

Opportunity

A way to take something and hide it. Weak controls create this.

Pressure

Debt, a quota, a habit, an expectation to hit.

Rationalization

"I'll pay it back." "They underpay me anyway."

A company can't control pressure or excuses. It can control opportunity. That is the job of internal control. SOX makes public companies document and test theirs.
C1 Principles of internal control

Seven principles, each with a real-world tell

1

Establish responsibilities

One cashier per drawer, so a shortage has a name.

2

Maintain adequate records

Prenumbered checks and invoices. A missing number gets noticed.

3

Insure assets, bond key employees

Bonding is insurance against theft by the people handling cash.

4

Separate recordkeeping from custody

Whoever holds the cash never records it.

5

Divide related transactions

Ordering, receiving and paying are three different people.

6

Apply technological controls

Registers, scanners, passwords, time clocks.

7

Regular, independent reviews

Audits by someone outside the process.

Limits: human error, collusion (two people working together beat separation of duties), and cost versus benefit.
C2 P1 Cash and its controls

What counts as cash, and three rules for guarding it

Cash

Currency, coins, checking and savings balances, and checks or money orders you've received.

Cash equivalents

Short-term, highly liquid investments with an original maturity of 3 months or less: T-bills, money market funds.

Trap: a 6-month CD bought today is not a cash equivalent. Count the maturity from the day you bought it.
1

Separate handling cash from recording it.

2

Deposit all receipts promptly, usually every day.

3

Pay by check or EFT. The only exception is small petty cash purchases.

Liquidity is how fast an asset turns into cash. Cash is the most liquid asset, which is exactly why it gets stolen.

P4 Voucher system

Six documents stand between an order and a check

1 · Department

Purchase requisition

A department asks purchasing to buy something.

2 · Purchasing

Purchase order

Sent to the vendor. Copies go to accounting, the department, and receiving, with the quantities left off.

3 · Vendor

Invoice

The vendor's bill for what it shipped.

4 · Receiving

Receiving report

Receiving's own count of what arrived. No quantities to copy, so they have to count.

5 · Accounting

Invoice approval

Checklist: requested? ordered? received? Price, math, terms right?

6 · Accounting

Voucher

The finished file. It authorizes recording the liability and paying it.

Why so many steps? Ordering, receiving and paying are split (principle 5), so nobody can buy something for themselves and approve the payment too.

P3 Bank reconciliation

Ask one question: who hasn't recorded it yet?

The bank doesn't know yet → bank side

Entries needed: never. Your books are already right.

+ Deposits in transit
− Outstanding checks
± Bank errors

Your books don't know yet → book side

Entries needed: every single one.

+ Interest earned, notes the bank collected, EFT receipts
− Service fees, NSF checks, EFT payments
± Book errors (compare "should be" to "recorded")
Book error trap: a $1,540 rent check recorded as $1,450. Your books took out $90 too little, so subtract $90 more from book. Dr Rent Expense 90, Cr Cash 90.

A1 Decision analysis

Days' sales uncollected: how long customers sit on your money

(Accounts receivable ÷ Net sales) × 365The answer is a number of days. Lower is better.

A/R $36,500, net sales $365,000

36,500 ÷ 365,000 = 0.1

0.1 × 365 = 36.5 days

Stop-early trap: 0.1 is a fraction of a year, not days. If your answer is under 1, you forgot the × 365.

P3 Estimating bad debts

Two methods. One gives the expense, one gives the target.

Percent of sales

Income statement approach. Credit sales × % is the expense. Ignore the allowance's current balance.

480,000 × 0.5% = 2,400 expense

Percent of receivables

Balance sheet approach. A/R × % (or an aging schedule) is where the allowance must end. The entry is the plug to get there.

A/R 80,000 × 3% = 2,400 target

A debit balance means last year's write-offs used up more than the reserve. You have to refill the hole first.

C2 Notes receivable

A written promise to pay, with interest

Maker signs and promises to pay. To them it's a note payable.
Payee gets paid. To them it's a note receivable.
Principal is the face amount. Maturity value = principal + interest.
Why take a note? It's stronger in court than an invoice, and it earns interest.
Principal × Rate × TimeTime = days ÷ 360, or months ÷ 12
$4,000 · 9% · 90 DAYS
4,000 × 9% = 360 a full year
360 × 90/360 = 360 × ¼ = 90

Maturity value: 4,090

No calculator trick: get the full year's interest first, then take the fraction.

A/R turnover: how many times a year receivables become cash

Net sales ÷ Average A/RAverage = (beginning + ending) ÷ 2. Higher is better.

Average A/R = (40,000 + 50,000) ÷ 2 = 45,000

540,000 ÷ 45,000 = 12 times

Big picture

Every long-term asset lives the same three-part life

Acquire

Work out its full cost and put it on the balance sheet.

→
Use

Spread that cost to expense over the years it helps earn revenue.

→
Dispose

Discard, sell, or trade it in. Record any gain or loss.

Trap 1: multiply by book value, not cost − salvage. Salvage is ignored in the multiplication.

C2 Partial years · changes in estimates

When the numbers don't fit neatly

Bought mid-year

Van bought April 1, straight-line $8,000 a year. April through December is 9 months.

8,000 × 9/12 = 6,000

Bought on the 1st–15th: count that month. On the 16th or later: start next month.

New estimate after 2 years

Book value is 36,000 − 16,000 = 20,000. Now you expect 3 more years and a $2,000 salvage.

used 16,000
18,000 ÷ 3
2k

(20,000 − 2,000) ÷ 3 = 6,000 a year

Never go back and fix past years. Start from today's book value and depreciate forward.

Quick testOnly keeps it working the way it already did → expense (revenue expenditure). Makes it better, bigger or last longer → capitalize (betterment or extraordinary repair).
P2 Disposals

Selling a plant asset in four steps

1Update depreciation to the sale date. Equipment: cost 30,000, accum. 21,000 at Dec 31, 6,000/yr. Sold July 1 → 6 months = 3,000.
2Remove the asset. Credit it at cost, debit all 24,000 of accumulated depreciation.
3Record what you got. Usually cash.
4Plug the gain or loss: received − book value (30,000 − 24,000 = 6,000).
book value 6,000
Skip step 1 and book value looks like 9,000. Every gain or loss comes out 3,000 off.

Ten traps your exam will try

  1. 6Cash short is a debit. Over is a credit.
  2. 6Petty cash check = fund − cash in the box.
  3. 6Bank-side items never get journal entries.
  4. 6Days' sales uncollected ends with × 365.
  5. 7Card sales: Sales is the full price.
  6. 7Allowance write-off: no change to realizable value or income.
  7. 7% of receivables gives a target. A debit balance gets added.
  8. 7Dishonored note → A/R, not bad debts.
  9. 8DDB multiplies book value, and stops at salvage.
  10. 8Update depreciation before computing a gain or loss.
